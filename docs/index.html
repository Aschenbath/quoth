Hacktoberfest 2026 · Ghost in the Machine

Quoth

Language models are trained on the words of the dead. I gave four small open-weight models the first forty words of 1,071 passages from 33 ghost stories and asked them to keep going. This is what they remember, word for word, and whom they forgot.

I · The birdIts only stock and store

In Poe’s poem a man asks a raven its name, and it answers with the one word it has. “Doubtless,” he says, “what it utters is its only stock and store, caught from some unhappy master.” I gave Gemma 4 E4B the first 40 words of The Raven and let it write. It wrote the next 709, those lines among them.

III · What it keepsThe doorway, not the house

In the ten classics, all four models lean the same way, and the lean sharpens as they grow: openings and the lines people quote rise above text no model has seen, while random pages from the middle of the same books stay close to it. The four random pages Gemma 4 E4B does recite are doorways of their own: the paragraph after the opening of A Christmas Carol, two passages of The Raven, which people learn by heart, and the a paragraph soon after Jekyll’s “man is not truly one, but truly two.”

V · Día de los MuertosAn ofrenda for the forgotten

On the Day of the Dead, families set out an ofrenda of marigolds, photographs and the food the dead loved, because the dead are only truly gone when no one remembers them. Many Victorian and Edwardian ghost stories were written by women who are rarely read now. I picked nine such books by hand, and six more posted to Project Gutenberg after the models’ data was collected. Thirteen of the fifteen writers are women, but this list was chosen, not sampled, so it says nothing about whether models remember women less. None of the sampled passages comes back: the longest run any of the four models manages on these books is five words, and no passage is more than 56% predicted. Here is a sentence from each, then the place where the forty given words stop, what the book says next, what the machine wrote instead, and a way to read the rest.

VI · How it worksMethod, and why it had to be open

Measuring memory

Each passage is 40 words of a book followed by the 50 words that really come next. The model reads the whole thing once, and at every position I check whether its single most likely next token is the true one. The unbroken run of hits from the start is what greedy decoding would reproduce, and I checked that by actually generating text for every passage with a run of five words or more. The three exceptions are two near-ties, where the model's two best guesses are almost equally likely and bfloat16 arithmetic breaks the tie differently in one pass than in step-by-step generation, and one edition difference: in A Christmas Carol the model wants a paragraph break before “Old Marley was as dead as a door-nail,” where this edition has none, and then goes on word for word.

A passage counts as recited when the run reaches ten words. No control passage came close.

That makes the counts a floor. In each of the three exceptions, generation keeps to the book at least as long: in one Raven passage it prints a semicolon where the book has an exclamation mark, which ends the strict count at 2 words, and then recites the rest of the stanza. The test starts at word 41, so a model can know a book's first sentence and still score nothing. Matching is exact, so the opening of Usher stops at 25 words because this edition prints two hyphens where the model writes a dash, and the opening of The Raven stops at two because the model writes a curly quotation mark. A line under ten words cannot count: Gemma 4 E4B predicts all of “man is not truly one, but truly two.” I noticed these after the protocol was frozen, so the counts stay as they were.

Reading on

Part I reads long generations with a second, looser ruler, added after the main run and kept apart from it. Words are compared without punctuation or case, and a slip of a word or two is passed over if the next three words line up again. It reads all 33 openings the same way, so none of them was picked for looking good; the other Raven passages it mentions were picked by hand.

Controls

  • 16 passages in the style of the books, written for this project and unpublished before the models were trained, so no model can have seen them. An AI (Claude) wrote them, and text written by an AI is easier for a model to predict, so they are a generous baseline.
  • Six ghost books first posted to Project Gutenberg in 2025 and 2026, after the models' training data was collected: human-written pages the models have probably never seen.
  • The sample, metrics and threshold were committed to git in PROTOCOL.md before the main run. What changed after that is listed at its end.

Counting copies

For each passage, the infini-gram API counts the ten words where the given text meets the continuation: in DCLM, a deduplicated open web corpus that stands in for Gemma's private data, and in OLMo 2's own pretraining data, which is itself mostly DCLM, so the two counts are close and are not independent evidence. Exact matching misses copies with other spellings or punctuation, so passages whose ten words span a line break are left out of every count.

On a laptop

Everything ran on one laptop CPU in bfloat16. Gemma 4 E4B's checkpoint is 15 GB and the machine had about 10 GB of RAM free, so the weights are memory-mapped straight from the safetensors file: the operating system pages in only what a forward pass touches. About 1.6 seconds per passage.

Why it had to be open

  • Open weights give the full probability of every token. A chat API returns text, and an instruction-tuned model paraphrases; neither tells you what the base model knows by heart.
  • Open data lets you ask why. OLMo 2's pretraining data, about four trillion tokens, is public and indexed by infini-gram, so every passage can be counted in the data the model was pretrained on. Gemma's training data is not public, so for Gemma an open web corpus is only a stand-in.
  • Open texts: the books are public domain, from Project Gutenberg, and the quoted lines come from Wikiquote's volunteers.
